# lab/rl: a research module on learning which item to feature

Murmur shows 14 theory lenses and 9 bedtime tracks in an order chosen by a person with
counseling training (the "expert order": `TOPIC_COMMON` in `js/lenses.js` and
`THEME_TRACK` in `js/bedtime.js`). This folder asks a narrow question about that order:
if the app learned from one user's own helpfulness ratings which single item to show
first, would that user be better off than with the expert order, and after how many
sessions?

Everything here is simulation and method. No user data is involved, and nothing in this
folder is loaded by the app.

## What is in the folder

| File | What it is |
|---|---|
| `POLICY_SPEC.md` | The contract shared with the in-app policy (`js/policy.js`): action, safety filter, priors, decision rule, reward, discounting, log format, constants. |
| `PREREG.md` | The study plan, written before the study was run. |
| `RESULTS.md` | The study's findings, generated by `run_study.py`. |
| `run_study.py` | Runs the preregistered study and the off-policy evaluation checks. |
| `murmur_rl/catalog.py` | Lenses, tracks, topics and expert orders, copied from the JavaScript (a test compares them). |
| `murmur_rl/safety.py` | The filter and the gate that every policy is called through, and an independent audit. |
| `murmur_rl/policies.py` | Expert order, epsilon-greedy, Thompson sampling (plain and discounted), the specified `murmur` policy, LinUCB. |
| `murmur_rl/env.py` | Synthetic users and four worlds. |
| `murmur_rl/ope.py` | IPS, self-normalised IPS, doubly robust, and an adaptively weighted estimator. |
| `murmur_rl/study.py` | The study and the checks as functions. |
| `tests/test_rl.py` | Unit tests. |

## How to run

From the repository root, with Python 3 and NumPy (matplotlib is optional and only draws
the figures; versions are pinned in `requirements.txt`):

    python3 -m unittest discover lab/rl/tests     # about half a minute
    python3 lab/rl/run_study.py --quick           # smoke test, about half a minute, writes results/quick/
    python3 lab/rl/run_study.py                   # the full study; rewrites results/ and RESULTS.md
    python3 lab/rl/run_study.py --from-csv        # redraw figures and RESULTS.md from the CSV files

The full study takes a few minutes on a laptop with four or more cores and about ten on a
single core. Results do not depend on the number of worker processes.

## The design

**The decision.** In each session the policy chooses ONE item to feature. The other
items stay in the expert order. Nothing is hidden and the person can pick anything.

**Safety before sampling.** The eligible items for the context are worked out first (for
a loss, only the five lenses suited to grief). Items outside that set are never given to
the policy, so they cannot receive probability. Then a gate is applied: if the person has
not opted in, if the risk check matched recently, if the latest mood check-in is low, or
if the topic is loss, the expert's first item is shown with probability 1 and the session
teaches the policy nothing.

**The model.** One Beta-Bernoulli posterior per context and item. The prior is worth 3
ratings and its mean follows the expert order, so with no data the policy's best guess is
the expert's first item.

**The decision rule.** With probability .8 the item with the highest posterior mean is
featured. With probability .2 the item is drawn from an exploration distribution that is
three quarters Thompson sampling and one quarter uniform. The full probability vector is
computed first and the item is drawn from it, so the probability that is logged is exactly
the probability that was used.

**The reward.** A rating of 4 or 5 on the existing "Was this helpful?" scale counts as
helpful. A session with no rating is recorded as missing and changes nothing.

**Drift.** Older ratings are down-weighted by .98 for each new rating in the same
context, which caps the evidence a context can hold at 50 ratings' worth.

The exact numbers are in `POLICY_SPEC.md`.

## Why a featured-item bandit and not a ranking

- **Data.** A person gives about one rating a day. A ranking of 14 items has about 87
  billion orders; one featured item has 14 choices. Only the second can be learned at
  all from tens of ratings.
- **A propensity that can be logged.** The probability of featuring one item is a
  single number that can be computed exactly and stored. The probability of a whole
  ranking is not practical to compute, and without it the logs cannot be evaluated later.
- **A reward that can be attributed.** One rating per session can be tied to one
  decision. Tying it to 14 positions needs a model of how people read a list, which
  nobody has for this app.
- **Bounded harm.** The worst the policy can do is put a less suitable but still
  eligible item first. The rest of the expert's order is intact and one tap away.
- **Legible.** "This one is shown first because you found it helpful before" can be
  explained to the person in one sentence.

## What can and cannot be claimed

Can be claimed:

- The safety filter and gate behave as specified in this code (tests, plus zero audit
  violations across the study's decisions).
- Logged propensities are exact for the mechanism that picks the item (tests).
- The off-policy estimators recover a known value from logs of a fixed logging policy
  in simulation, with the bias and variance reported in `RESULTS.md`.
- How the learned policies compare with the expert order under the stated, invented
  assumptions about users.

Cannot be claimed:

- That personalisation helps, or does not help, real users. The simulator's users were
  not fitted to anyone.
- That a "helpful" rating means the person was helped. It is a single self-report
  given straight after the exercise, and people who found a session unhelpful rate
  less often, which pushes every estimate from real ratings upward.
- Anything clinical. A lens exercise is psychoeducation, and a rating of one is not
  an outcome measure.
- Confidence intervals from adaptively collected logs. Once the policy adapts, each
  propensity depends on earlier ratings, and the usual intervals are no longer
  justified. `ope.py` includes a check and a variance-stabilised estimator for a simple
  case; intervals for the contextual case are out of scope.
- That the in-app JavaScript matches this Python. It has to pass its own tests against
  section 10 of `POLICY_SPEC.md`.

## Privacy

The policy runs on the device. Its state and its decision log live in the same on-device
storage as the rest of Murmur. The log holds the context label (for example "work"),
the eligible item ids, the probabilities, the item featured, the item used and the
rating. It holds no worry text, no answers and no voice measures. Nothing is sent
anywhere. The log leaves the device only if the person exports it, and deleting all data
deletes it. A topic label with a timestamp is still personal information, so an exported
log should be treated like the JSON backup: unencrypted, and the person's own to share
or not.

## Ethics

- **Exploration is opt-in and off by default.** Without it the app shows the expert
  order and logs nothing. The setting should say in plain words that the app will
  sometimes show a different lens first in order to learn, and it can be switched off
  at any time.
- **No exploration when someone is struggling.** Low mood, a recent risk match and
  the loss topic always get the expert's first item.
- **The owner only, until reviewed.** Running this on the owner's own device is
  self-experimentation. Offering exploration to anyone else, or analysing anyone
  else's exported log, is research with human participants and needs review by an
  institutional review board (or the local equivalent) and informed consent first.
- **No engagement objective.** The reward is the person's own helpfulness rating.
  Time in the app, streaks and return visits are not rewards and must not become ones.
- **The expert order is the default to beat, not an obstacle.** If the learned policy
  does not clearly beat it, the right outcome is to keep the expert order.

## From here to a micro-randomized trial

An adaptive policy is the wrong first step for learning anything about people, because
its logs are hard to analyse (see the adaptive check in `RESULTS.md`). The order that
keeps the inference simple is:

1. **Fixed randomization first.** For consenting participants and after ethics review,
   randomize at each ungated session with probabilities that never change: for example
   the expert's first item with probability .5 and each of the expert's next four items
   with .125. This is a micro-randomized trial. The decision point is the session, the
   proximal outcome is the rating (and whether one was given), and availability is
   "not gated". Because the probabilities are fixed and known, ordinary weighted and
   centered analyses of the causal excursion effect apply, and so do the intervals from
   `ope.py`.
2. **Use the trial to check the assumptions this simulation had to invent**: how much
   people differ in which item helps, how fast that drifts, how often the featured item
   is the one used, and how missingness depends on the outcome.
3. **Evaluate the learned policy offline on the trial's logs** with the estimators here,
   before it chooses anything for anyone.
4. **Only then consider the adaptive policy**, with the same log format, a floor on
   every propensity, and a prespecified comparison against the expert order.

The decision log in `POLICY_SPEC.md` section 8 already has the fields step 1 needs:
context, eligible items, the full probability vector, the item featured, the item used,
the rating, and the reason when a decision was gated.
